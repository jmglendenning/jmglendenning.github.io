5. Discuss — linkage methods and algorithms

  1. Linkage methods.

    1. Deterministic linkage. Two records match when chosen fields agree exactly, or agree under a fixed rule. What does a typo, a married name, or a missing birth date do to that rule? Who is most likely to be missed?

A fixed rule leaves no room for error, so one bad field breaks the match. A typo or nickname drops a true pair below the cutoff. A married name splits one woman into two records, and if surname is a blocking key the pair is never compared at all. A missing birth date leaves the rule nothing to evaluate. Rules also give no threshold for trading false matches against missed ones, and loosening them adds false matches among common names. The people missed are those with messy or changing records: women who changed surnames, people with hyphenated or transliterated names (Tokle and Bender note Asian names were harder to match uniquely), frequent movers, and anyone with incomplete records. Because these misses cluster by sex, ethnicity and class, the linked file is a selected sample.

ii. *Probabilistic linkage.* Each pair of records gets a probability of being a match, from how often fields agree among true matches and among non-matches. Where does that probability come from when no one has labelled the true matches?

In the Fellegi–Sunter model, each field comparison has an m-probability (agreement among true matches) and a u-probability (agreement among non-matches), and their ratio is the match weight. With no labelled matches, match status is treated as a latent class, and the probabilities are estimated by EM from the comparison patterns themselves. fastLink does this and treats missing values as missing at random (Enamorado et al., 2019). The probability is therefore only as good as the model’s assumptions of conditionally independent fields and ignorable missingness. Turning probabilities into links requires a threshold, often with a clerical-review band. Raising it trades false matches for missed ones, so the linkage should be validated against a hand-reviewed sample.

iii. *Statistical matching vs. exact matching.* Exact matching looks for the same person or unit in two files. Statistical matching combines files that share no units, by pairing similar units. What can each support you in claiming — about individuals, and about populations?

Exact matching finds the same unit in both files. It supports individual-level claims and population-level claims about joint distributions, subject to linkage error. Statistical matching pairs similar but different units, so it produces a synthetic file. It supports no claims about individuals, and only population claims that rest on an untestable assumption: the variables unique to each file are conditionally independent given the matching variables. When that fails, relationships across the two files are biased toward zero.

  1. Linkage algorithms. Trace one pair of records through the steps of ch. 3: preprocessing (standardizing names, dates, addresses), blocking, comparison (exact, or string distance such as Jaro–Winkler), classification by threshold, and enforcing one-to-one links. Which step do you think does most to decide the result? Which is least reported?

Tracing one pair. Take two records for the same woman. In a voter file she is “MARIA GARCIA-LOPEZ, 07/03/1985, 12 Elm Street, Apt 4.” In a later administrative file she is “Maria Lopez, 1985-03-07, 12 Elm St #4.”

Preprocessing standardizes both records: case, punctuation, and address abbreviations (“Street” to “St”, “Apt 4” and “#4” to a common unit field), with names parsed into first, last and middle. It also has to resolve the date. Read “07/03/1985” as US month/day, and it becomes July 3, which no longer matches the March 7 in the second file. Read it as day/month, and it agrees. Nothing in the data settles that; a coding choice does.

Blocking then decides whether the pair is ever compared. If the block is last name plus birth year, the records land in different blocks (“GARCIA-LOPEZ” vs. “LOPEZ”), and the true match is lost before any scoring happens. Blocking on birth year plus ZIP code keeps them together.

Comparison scores each field. First name agrees exactly. Last name gets a partial Jaro–Winkler score, because “LOPEZ” is only part of “GARCIA-LOPEZ”. Date and address agree after standardization.

Classification sums these into a match weight and compares it with a threshold. A clean surname agreement would have cleared it easily; the partial surname score may leave the pair in the clerical-review band.

One-to-one enforcement finally resolves conflicts. If another “Maria Lopez” in the same block scores slightly higher, the Hungarian assignment can give her this record, and our true pair loses even after passing the threshold.

Which step does most to decide the result.

Blocking. Every other step can be revisited: a pair scored badly can be rescored, and a threshold can be moved. But a pair that blocking separates is never compared, so no later step can recover it. Its failures are also systematic. Blocking on surname drops exactly the people whose names changed, and the chapter flags name changes and moves as the cases exact blocking keys miss. Preprocessing comes close, since it determines what the blocking keys contain.

Which is least reported.

Preprocessing. Papers usually report the blocking scheme, the comparison functions and the threshold, but rarely the standardization rules: which nicknames were mapped, how hyphenated and compound surnames were split, which date format was assumed, how addresses were parsed. These choices often live in undocumented cleaning scripts. Yet they set the inputs to every later step, and they fall unevenly on people with non-Anglo names, compound surnames or nonstandard addresses. Blocking is a close second: studies often say what they blocked on but almost never estimate how many true matches the blocking excluded.

A linked file is produced, not found. Linkage creates records that existed in neither source. Its errors are false matches and missed matches — and they are not random: they fall harder on people with common names, changed names, or incomplete records. Come ready to say how you would report a linkage, and how you would know it worked.
