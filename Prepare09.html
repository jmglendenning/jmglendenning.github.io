5. Discuss — a comment section as data

  1. Who writes YouTube comments, and who reads them? Who never appears?
  2. What edits them — channel moderation, held-for-review filters, YouTube’s ranking (order = "relevance"), deletions?
  3. What selects the comments you can collect: the search ranking, the video, the channel, your max_results, the day you ran it?
  4. The API terms allow you to keep this data 30 days. What does that mean for reproducibility — and for your website?

a. Who writes, who reads, who never appears

A small, self-selected minority writes comments, and a few accounts write most of them. Commenters lean toward people with strong views, and they include spam accounts, bots, coordinated campaigns, and the channel owner. Many more people read than write. Most viewers never comment, so the comment section measures the engaged tail, not the audience.

Several groups never appear:

  • Viewers who watch silently.
  • People who don’t use YouTube at all. They skew older and toward places with poor broadband, which matters for a siting question.
  • Anyone whose comment was removed or held.
  • Every viewer of a video with comments disabled. Made-for-Kids videos have comments turned off automatically.

For your project, the people who live next to a proposed facility may be far less visible than national commentators. Because comments carry no location, you can’t tell the two apart.

b. What edits them

Comments pass through several filters before you see them:

  • The commenter: People edit their own comments, which is why updated_at exists.
  • The creator: Creators can delete comments, hide users, block words, and hold comments for review. Held comments aren’t public, so the API never returns them.
  • YouTube: Automated spam filters hold comments, and policy enforcement removes them.
  • Ranking: The call above uses the API default, order = "time", so truncation favors recent comment threads. If order = "relevance" is requested, truncation favors comments YouTube ranks as relevant.
  • Partial replies: commentThreads.list returns top-level threads and may include only some replies. A fuller collection would follow up with comments.list for replies.
  • Timing: Deletion can happen after you collect a comment. A stored corpus can therefore contain comments that are no longer public, which matters for (d).

c. What selects what you can collect

Each layer narrows the sample:

  • Search: search.list returns a relevance-ranked sample, not a complete list. It is shaped by the query, regionCode, relevanceLanguage, pagination, and the day of collection.
  • The video and channel: Their audience, moderation norms, and whether comments are enabled all shape what is available.
  • max_results and paging: With the default time ordering, stopping after 100 selects recent top-level threads, not a random 100. With relevance ordering, it selects the threads YouTube ranks most highly.
  • The day you ran it: Comments keep arriving, and some get edited or deleted. nrow(cmts) can differ from get_video_stats() because the former counts retrieved top-level threads and the latter reports a broader total that includes replies.
  • Quota: Quota and endpoint-specific call limits constrain how much can be collected.

For this exercise, the corpus is a sample of publicly visible top-level comments, at the time of collection, on the latest upload from one fixed channel. In a search-based project, YouTube’s ranking of videos adds another selection layer. Neither corpus is a sample of public opinion; its frame is defined by the query or channel, video-selection rule, API filters, collection time, and result limit.

d. The 30-day rule: reproducibility and your website

Section III.E.4 of the policy is specific:

  • Data collected with only an API key is “Non-Authorized Data.”
  • You may store limited amounts of it temporarily, for no more than 30 days. After that you must delete it or refresh it.
  • The same limit applies to statistics like view counts and subscriber counts.
  • Stored data must reasonably match what YouTube currently shows.

Three consequences:

  • You can’t publish a frozen comment corpus for others to rerun. Reproducibility moves from sharing data to sharing code, queries, collection dates, and a clear description of the sampling process.
  • Re-collection won’t match exactly. Re-pulling the same IDs later can return a different set because comments are added, deleted, or edited.
  • The research design is affected. A plan for frozen, hashed weekly snapshots or a sealed estimation sample conflicts with the 30-day refresh-or-delete rule for Non-Authorized Data. Section III.E.4.h also restricts derived metrics from API data. The project should confirm what is permitted under the applicable terms or a research authorization before retaining long-term snapshots or publishing derived scores.

For the website:

  • Keep Parquet files and raw comment text out of the public repository with .gitignore.
  • Check rendered output carefully: Quarto’s _freeze folder and rendered HTML can preserve printed comment text or API statistics.
  • Publish the collection code, queries, dates, and sampling description. Publish aggregate or derived results only after confirming that the applicable YouTube terms or research authorization permit them.
