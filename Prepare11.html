The Census Geocoder places the address at 32.97833, -96.74549; OpenStreetMap places it at 32.98514, -96.74979. The two points are 857 metres apart.

Neither point is the building. The Census point is an interpolation: the Bureau found the TIGER street segment of W Campbell Rd whose address range contains 800 (line not returned, side not returned) and placed the point along it in proportion to where 800 falls in that range. Address ranges include every possible number, whether or not a building stands there, so the point lands by the road, not on a roof. The OpenStreetMap point is whatever OSM object matched — here building university — which may be an address point, a building outline or the campus as a whole.

Which is “right” depends on the use. To join the address to census tables, both are right if they fall in the same tract, and here they do. To measure distance to a facility or to map a building, neither is right without checking it against imagery or a parcel point. And 800 W Campbell Rd is one address for a whole campus of buildings.

4. Discuss — where a point comes from

  1. Geocodes. A geocoder turns an address into a point by matching it to a reference file — for the Census Bureau, street segments with address ranges — and interpolating. Which addresses fail to match: rural routes, PO boxes, new subdivisions, apartments, addresses in Spanish or Vietnamese? If the failures are not random, what happens to an analysis of the matched records? And whose server sees the addresses you send?
  2. Interactive maps. A choropleth chooses a unit, a variable, a rate or a count, breaks and colors; an interactive map adds a basemap, zoom and pop-ups. Which of these choices changes what the reader concludes? What does publishing a zoomable map of individual points reveal that a table would not?
  3. Location services. Your phone places itself by GPS, Wi-Fi and cell towers; apps pass the points to SDKs and data brokers, and researchers buy “mobility data.” De Montjoye et al. found four spatio-temporal points enough to single out 95 percent of people. Can location data be anonymized? Who consented, and to what?
  4. Spatial modeling. Tobler: near things are more related than distant things. When neighboring tracts resemble each other, what goes wrong with OLS standard errors? Is spatial dependence a nuisance to correct (an error model) or a process to model (a lag model)? And would the answer survive changing the unit — tracts, ZIP codes, precincts?

a. Geocodes

The addresses that fail are the ones the reference file does not describe as a position on a street:

  • Rural routes: “RR 2 Box 15” names a delivery route, not a place on a street, so no address range contains it.
  • PO boxes: they locate a box at a post office, not where anyone lives; at best they match to the post office.
  • New subdivisions: streets and ranges enter the reference file after they are built, so new addresses fail until it catches up.
  • Apartments: the unit number is dropped, so every unit lands on one point; complexes on private internal streets may not match at all.
  • Addresses in Spanish or Vietnamese: word order, diacritics and unfamiliar street types break the parser before matching even starts.

These failures are not random. They cluster in rural, newly built, renter-heavy and immigrant neighborhoods. The matched records are then a selected sample: counts and rates in those places are biased down, and because the bias is itself spatial, it can look like a real pattern on a map. Report the match rate overall and by area, read the unmatched records, and never treat NA as missing at random.

Whose server: method = "census" sends every address to the Census Bureau; method = "osm" sends it to the OpenStreetMap Foundation’s Nominatim server, whose usage policy allows at most one request a second and asks users not to send personal data. Each request also carries your IP address. For confidential records — patients, voters, survey respondents — that can breach a data-use agreement or an IRB protocol; such addresses should be geocoded on your own machine, against a local copy of the reference files.

b. Interactive maps

The choices that change the conclusion are the ones that change the pattern:

  • Unit: the same data mapped by tract, ZIP code or county can show different clusters (the modifiable areal unit problem). Large, empty rural units dominate the eye.
  • Rate or count: a map of counts is mostly a map of population. Rates fix that, but rates in small units are noisy, so the extremes are often the smallest places.
  • Breaks: quantiles, equal intervals and natural breaks can make the same values look uniform or polarized.
  • Colors: a diverging scheme asserts a meaningful midpoint; a sequential one does not.

The interactive layer mostly changes what the reader believes about precision. A basemap and zoom invite reading the data at building scale, finer than a street-centerline geocode supports, and pop-ups attach attributes to single features.

A zoomable map of individual points shows where, not just how many. On a basemap each point resolves to a house, and a reader can find the person — a crime victim, a patient, a commenter — without a name on the map. A table by tract shows only counts. The safer options are to aggregate, to displace points at random within a set radius, to suppress small counts, or to cap the zoom.

c. Location services

A phone places itself by GPS (a few metres outdoors), by Wi-Fi (matching nearby networks against databases of their locations) and by cell towers (hundreds of metres or more). Apps with location permission collect the points; advertising and analytics code embedded in them (SDKs) forwards them; brokers combine them into “mobility data” — a device ID, a position and a time — and sell it.

Removing the name does not anonymize a trace, because the trace is the identifier. De Montjoye et al. (2013) studied about 1.5 million phone users over 15 months, with location known only to the cell tower and time to the hour. Four points picked out 95 percent of them, and making the data coarser helped less than expected: even at much lower resolution, about half could still be singled out. A pseudonymized trace is personal data. Real protection means giving up detail: aggregated counts with small cells suppressed, or formal methods such as differential privacy.

Consent was given to an app, for a purpose: a weather forecast, directions. The chain to SDKs, brokers and researchers sits in privacy policies few read, and nobody consented to research. People who appear only because they were near someone else’s phone consented to nothing.

d. Spatial modeling

When neighboring tracts resemble each other in ways the model does not explain, the residuals are spatially autocorrelated and the observations are not independent. Each tract carries part of its neighbors’ information, so the effective sample is smaller than n. OLS still treats every tract as independent: its standard errors are too small and its t-statistics too large, so it finds effects that are not there. If the dependence is in the outcome itself, OLS coefficients are biased as well, because the model omits the neighbors’ outcome.

Whether dependence is a nuisance or a process is a question about the world before it is a test. An error model fits dependence that comes from something omitted that is clustered in space — or from boundaries that cut across the real process; that is a nuisance to correct. A lag model fits an outcome in one place that affects its neighbors — diffusion, imitation, spillovers; that is a process to model. Moran’s I on the OLS residuals shows whether there is dependence, and Lagrange multiplier tests help choose between the two, but the two are hard to tell apart in data. In R, spdep builds the neighbor weights and tests; spatialreg fits both models (lagsarlm(), errorsarlm()).

The answer may not survive a change of unit. Aggregation changes both the strength of autocorrelation and the coefficients (the modifiable areal unit problem). The units are not neutral either: tracts are drawn around population (about 4,000 people), ZIP codes follow mail routes, and precincts change between elections. Model at the unit where the process operates, and report whether the result holds at others.

Important

A location is produced, not found. An address is recorded by an office, a point by a matching algorithm or a phone, a boundary by an agency for its own purposes. Each step adds error that falls on some places and people more than others. Come ready to trace one point on a map back through those steps.

Tracing one point

The point from 3b, traced back:

  1. The address. A local addressing authority assigned the number, and the Postal Service delivers to it. 800 W Campbell Rd is one address for a whole campus.
  2. The point. The Census Geocoder matched it to TIGER line not returned and interpolated along that segment’s address range, assuming the numbers are evenly spaced. OpenStreetMap matched a different object, 857 metres away.
  3. The boundary. The point falls in Census Tract 9802 (GEOID 48113980200), a boundary the Census Bureau draws for statistical tabulation and redraws each decade. Tract boundaries often follow roads, so a point interpolated onto a street centerline can fall on either side of one.

Each step’s error is uneven: one address for many buildings, an interpolation that assumes regular spacing, and a boundary drawn for counting people rather than for my question.
