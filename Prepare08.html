4. Discuss —

Analytics of Text Data

a. How are text data generated?

For the hearings: who spoke, who edited, who printed, and what decided which ones you could collect?

Senate Foreign Relations Committee hearings

The hearings contain statements and testimony from senators, witnesses, and other participants. The published documents are products of a formal congressional process, including transcription, editing, and publication by the Government Publishing Office (GPO). Consequently, they are not necessarily verbatim records of everything said during a hearing.

Our collection is limited to hearings returned by the GovInfo API query, sorted by publication date. This introduces selection bias because the ten most recently published hearings are not necessarily the ten most recently conducted. The corpus also excludes hearings that have not yet been published or are unavailable through the API.

b. Topics —

what would a topic model find in ten SFRC hearings, and what would convince you it found something real?

Senate Foreign Relations Committee hearings

A topic model might identify recurring subjects such as national security, diplomatic relations, international conflicts, foreign assistance, or economic sanctions. However, these are plausible topics rather than findings from the ten hearings.

With only ten documents, topic modeling at the hearing level may produce unstable results. A larger number of observations could be obtained by dividing hearings into individual statements or testimony segments, although this would require careful segmentation.

I would validate the topics by examining the most influential words and representative passages, then comparing them against hearing titles, agendas, and substantive content.

c. Sentiment —

what would a dictionary make of diplomatic testimony? What does it miss?

Senate Foreign Relations Committee hearings

A dictionary-based sentiment model might classify testimony containing words such as “conflict,” “threat,” or “crisis” as negative, even when a speaker is supporting a diplomatic initiative. Similarly, positive language about international cooperation does not necessarily indicate approval of a particular foreign policy.

Dictionary methods have difficulty interpreting context, negation, sarcasm, and the subject toward which an opinion is directed.

d. Positions and polarity —

could you place speakers on a scale from their words? Against what would you validate it?

Senate Foreign Relations Committee hearings

Speakers could potentially be positioned along a defined policy dimension using their statements. However, different hearings may address unrelated foreign policy questions, making a single ideological scale difficult to interpret.

Wordfish could estimate relative positions from word frequencies, but the resulting dimension would require substantive validation. I would first identify a specific policy issue and restrict the analysis to relevant statements.

The estimated positions could then be compared with independently coded statements, recorded votes where relevant, or documented positions on the issue.

5. Bring — text sources for your team project

List three candidate sources (web pages, government documents, news, platform posts, transcripts). For each, one line on:

  1. who produced it, for whom, and why;

  2. the unit (a speech, a post, a hearing) and roughly how many;

  3. the route — API, bulk download, scraping, or manual — and the permission that covers it;

  4. one gap: who or what the source leaves out.

1. YouTube Comments

Producer and audience: YouTube users produce comments for other viewers, video creators, and participants in online discussions. Comments may express opinions about data center development, electricity costs, environmental concerns, and local economic impacts.

Unit and quantity: The primary unit is an individual comment. We will collect comments from videos identified using data-center-related search terms. The final number of comments will depend on the number of relevant videos, API availability, and collection limits. We have currently captured 800,000 comments for review over thousands of videos.

Collection and permission: We will use the YouTube Data API v3 with an authorized API key. Requests will follow Google’s API policies and quota restrictions.

Known gap: YouTube commenters are self-selected and cannot be assumed to represent U.S. public opinion. Comments may also be removed, moderated, or unavailable through the API.

2. Congressional Candidate Websites

Producer and audience: U.S. House and Senate candidates and their campaign organizations produce official websites to communicate policy positions to voters, supporters, journalists, and potential donors.

Unit and quantity: The primary unit is a webpage or policy statement discussing data centers. We intend to examine the websites of candidates competing in the 2026 congressional elections. The number of relevant documents will depend on how many candidates address the issue. We have already examined tens of thousands of pages over 1,100+ websites.

Collection and permission: Campaign websites will be examined for references to data centers and related terms. Relevant pages may be collected through respectful web scraping or manual collection, subject to each website’s terms and applicable access restrictions.

Known gap: Campaign websites represent curated political communications rather than complete records of candidate opinions. A candidate who does not mention data centers cannot automatically be classified as neutral or opposed.

3. News Articles

Producer and audience: Journalists, editors, and news organizations produce articles for general audiences, local communities, policymakers, and subscribers. Coverage may describe proposed data centers, public opposition, environmental concerns, and political responses.

Unit and quantity: The primary unit is a news article. We plan to collect relevant articles published during the 2026 election cycle. The final count will depend on search terms, source coverage, and access to article text. We expect to view several thousand news articles over November 30, 2025 through November 3, 2026.

Collection and permission: Media Cloud is a potential discovery source for identifying relevant news coverage. Full-text collection would require access through permitted APIs, publisher websites, or other authorized sources. Article metadata and full text must be distinguished.

Known gap: News coverage reflects editorial decisions and newsworthiness rather than the prevalence of public attitudes. Local controversies may receive extensive coverage, while less visible developments may receive little attention.
